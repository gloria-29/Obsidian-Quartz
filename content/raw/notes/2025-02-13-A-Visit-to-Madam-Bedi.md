Markdown Content: My friend Sukrit invited me to India.  
我的朋友苏克里特邀请我去印度。

His mother lived in Delhi. He said I should get out of England and give my eyes something new to look at. He wouldn’t be there—he was trapped in a biology lab at Stanford—but his mother would look after me. I could stay as long as I liked.  
他母亲住在德里。他说我应该离开英国，给我的眼睛一些新的东西。他不会去那里，他被困在斯坦福大学的生物实验室里，但他母亲会照顾我。我想待多久就待多久。

The invitation confused me. I could not imagine why I would go to a country that was not my country, to live with a mother who was not my mother. I pawed at the idea, then dismissed it. I did not want to go east; I wanted to go west. I was waiting for my family to reclaim me.  
这个邀请让我很困惑。我无法想象我为什么要去一个不是我祖国的国家，和一个不是我母亲的母亲生活在一起。我想了想，又打消了这个念头。我不想去东边，我想去西边。我在等待家人来接我。

I don’t know where the hope lived or what it lived on. I had been estranged from my father for a year by then, but I was still telling myself that the estrangement was temporary, that the breach would heal. My mother was key.  
我不知道希望在哪里，也不知道它靠什么活着。那时，我已经与父亲疏远了一年，但我仍然告诉自己，疏远是暂时的，裂痕会愈合。母亲是关键。  
I thought she would convince my father, soften his heart. That’s how it happens in the Bible, when two souls fall out of kinship. God softens a heart. I wasn’t religious, not the way my father had raised me to be, but I believed in the softening of hearts. So I waited.  
我以为她会说服父亲，软化他的心。圣经》里就是这么写的，当两个灵魂失去了亲情。上帝会软化人心我不信教，不像我父亲教我的那样 但我相信人心会软化所以我在等待  
For a letter. A phone call. I imagined my father saying, “Come home.” Of course I could not go to India. When my father called, I had to be ready.

Months passed. The seasons changed. I wrote my mother every few weeks and she answered. She wrote as if everything were all right, as if we were not estranged. She told me about her days, her shopping trips with my sister, the steady expansion of her herbal business.  
几个月过去了。季节变换。我每隔几周给母亲写信，她都会回信。她在信中说一切都好，好像我们并没有疏远。她告诉我她的日子，她和我姐姐一起去购物，她的草药生意在稳步扩大。  
From these lines of text, I extracted the sensation of being a daughter.  
从这些文字中，我体会到了作为女儿的感觉。

Then another year had passed with silence from my father. It was difficult, then, to keep believing that we would reconcile, but equally difficult to give up that belief.  
后来又过了一年，父亲一直保持沉默。那时，我很难继续相信我们会和好，但同样也很难放弃这个信念。  
I did not know how to live with the loss of my parents, or the bitterness that the loss was introducing into my life.  
我不知道如何面对失去父母的痛苦，也不知道如何面对失去父母给我的生活带来的苦涩。

I must have seemed bewildered. Somehow my friends knew.  
我看起来一定很困惑。不知怎的，我的朋友们知道了。  
It must have been present in the tone of my voice, its vinegary flavor, because one night, when I was speaking on the phone with Sukrit, who was settled in California, whom I had not seen in the whole of that year, the conversation faltered and into the gap he repeated his strange invitation.  
因为有一天晚上，当我和定居在加利福尼亚的苏克里特通电话时，谈话突然中断了，他在电话间隙重复了他那奇怪的邀请。  
He said I should go to India. And this time when he said it, without knowing the reason, I said that I would go.  
他说我应该去印度。这次他这么说的时候，我还不知道原因，就说我要去。

I had met Sukrit three years before, at the University of Cambridge, where we were both graduate students. He was a biologist. I was a historian. It would be difficult to contrive two people with less in common.  
三年前，我在剑桥大学认识了苏克里特，当时我们都是研究生。他是生物学家。我是历史学家。很难想象两个人的共同点会更少。  
Sukrit was from Delhi, the eldest son of a grand Sikh family, with a royal lineage on his mother’s side. His grandfather was an inspector general. His mother was a high-powered government official.  
苏克里特来自德里，是一个锡克族大家族的长子，母亲那边有皇室血统。他的祖父是一名督察长。他的母亲是一位位高权重的政府官员。  
Sukrit had grown up with guards and servants, and he carried himself like someone who had. Like someone who belonged at that school.  
苏克里特是在卫兵和仆人的陪伴下长大的，他的举止就像一个卫兵和仆人。就像一个属于那所学校的人。

If Sukrit’s presence at Cambridge had a whiff of inevitability, my own presence there was nearly absurd. I was out of context, a hayseed blowing among the Gothic spires and ornate marble statuary.  
如果说苏克里特出现在剑桥有一种不可避免的味道，那么我自己出现在剑桥则近乎荒谬。我与剑桥的环境格格不入，就像一个在哥特式尖塔和华丽的大理石雕像间穿梭的乡巴佬。

I had been born in Idaho, the youngest of seven children, and raised at the base of a mountain called Buck’s Peak. It was a lonely upbringing. My father said that those who follow the Lord will be shunned. He said that it was our privilege to be shunned by the world.  
我出生在爱达荷州，是七个孩子中最小的一个，在一座名叫巴克峰的山脚下长大。那是一个孤独的成长环境。我父亲说，追随主的人将会被避开。他说，被世人躲避是我们的特权。

He was an ideologue, a sectarian, wholly devoted to his singular religion. The government was corrupt. Public education was brainwashing, a satanic instrument of a fallen world.  
他是一个意识形态主义者，是一个宗派主义者，全心全意地信奉他那独特的宗教。政府是腐败的。公共教育是洗脑，是堕落世界的撒旦工具。  
Modern medicine—doctors and hospitals and pharmaceuticals, what he termed the “medical establishment”—was profane and godless.  
现代医学--医生、医院和药品，也就是他所说的 &quot;医疗机构&quot;--是亵渎神灵的。  
People of faith relied on herbalism, Dad said, so my mother brewed tinctures of black cohosh and blue vervain, oat straw and blessed thistle, what Dad called “God’s pharmacy.” He was opposed to antibiotics.  
爸爸说，有信仰的人依赖草药，所以我母亲用黑升麻和蓝马鞭草、燕麦秸秆和福蓟草制成酊剂，爸爸称之为 &quot;上帝的药房&quot;。他反对抗生素  
Once, when I was seven or so, my father told me that if I swallowed a single tablet of aspirin, my children’s children would be deformed in the womb. “God visits the sins of the fathers on the children,” he said.  
有一次，在我七岁左右的时候，父亲告诉我，如果我吞下一片阿司匹林，我孩子的孩子就会在子宫里畸形。他说，&quot;上帝会把父亲的罪孽加在孩子身上&quot;。

I don’t know what the source of my father’s paranoia was, only that it seemed to touch everything. Like most of my siblings, I was never put in school. I was educated at home, according to my father’s beliefs.  
我不知道父亲妄想症的根源是什么，只知道它似乎触及了一切。像我的大多数兄弟姐妹一样，我从未上过学。按照父亲的观念，我是在家里接受教育的。  
I was also born at home, delivered by a midwife, and my birth was not registered. When I was nine, I was issued a delayed certificate of birth, but the birth date that ended up on the form was an approximation. We did not know my birthday. I still don’t know it.  
我也是在家里出生的，由一名助产士接生，没有进行出生登记。九岁那年，我拿到了一份延迟出生证明，但表格上的出生日期只是一个近似值。我们不知道我的生日。我现在也不知道。

When I was seventeen, I left the mountain and enrolled at Brigham Young University. It was the first time that I had set foot in a classroom, and immediately I dedicated myself to education.  
17 岁那年，我离开大山，进入杨百翰大学学习。这是我第一次踏进教室，我立刻全身心地投入到教育事业中。  
For the next decade I clawed at the world, trying to take from it what I needed to remake myself, to reverse the ignorance and vulnerability of my early years. My philosophy back then, my whole posture relative to the world, was one of discipline and self-sovereignty.  
在接下来的十年里，我一直在这个世界上摸爬滚打，试图从中获取重塑自我所需的东西，扭转早年的无知和脆弱。那时，我的哲学，我对世界的整个姿态，就是自律和自我主宰。  
I was a rationalist. I thought any problem could be solved by the application of will.  
我是一个理性主义者。我认为任何问题都可以通过意志来解决。

I climbed. I climbed from that mountain in Idaho to the University of Cambridge, where, one afternoon in my second year of graduate work, I met Sukrit, a princely Indian biologist who drank whiskey neat and laughed at his own jokes. We formed a kinship, us two.  
我爬过山。我从爱达荷州的那座山爬到了剑桥大学，在那里，研究生二年级的一个下午，我遇到了苏克里特，一个喝威士忌不加水、笑自己笑话的印度生物学家。我们俩结下了不解之缘。  
The bond was instant. Sometimes it happens like that. You meet someone, and for no obvious reason you both recognize something in the other, something beyond gender or nationality or class or race or religion.  
我们的关系一拍即合。有时事情就是这样。你遇到一个人，没有任何明显的原因，你们都能在对方身上发现一些东西，一些超越性别、国籍、阶级、种族或宗教的东西。  
Within a month, Sukrit and I were spending every evening together, with our small circle of friends, drinking whiskey and mangling philosophy and singing sea chanteys at three in the morning outside the resplendent Great Gate of Trinity College.  
一个月内，我和苏克里特每天晚上都和我们的小伙伴们在一起，喝着威士忌，咀嚼着哲学，凌晨三点还在三一学院辉煌的大门外唱着海上歌谣。

I don’t remember telling Sukrit about the mountain, about my father or the estrangement. I don’t remember telling him that my own mother had refused to see me. Back then I kept my secrets or thought I did.  
我不记得告诉过苏克里特关于山的事，关于我父亲或疏远的事。我不记得告诉过他，我的亲生母亲拒绝见我。那时我保守着秘密，或者说我以为我保守着秘密。  
Still, it is apparent to me now that he knew, and that probably this was the reason he told me to go to India. I had lost my own mother. Perhaps he thought he could lend me his.  
不过，现在我觉得他显然是知道的，这可能就是他让我去印度的原因。我失去了自己的母亲。也许他认为可以把他的母亲借给我。

The carpet at the Indira Gandhi International Airport was varicolored and vaguely modern, an intricate pattern of quadrilaterals, shades of mustard yellow and marmalade. The arrivals hall was wide and packed with travellers.  
英迪拉-甘地国际机场的地毯色彩斑斓，隐约透着现代气息，是由四边形、芥末黄和橘子酱色调组成的复杂图案。到达大厅很宽敞，挤满了旅客。  
I moved through immigration and saw a man clutching a sheet of white paper on which a name was scrawled. I stepped forward and nodded at the paper.  
我通过入境处，看到一个人手里拿着一张白纸，上面写着一个名字。我走上前去，对着白纸点了点头。

“Madam Bedi?” he said. &quot;贝迪女士？他说

“Yes,” I said. “Satbir Bedi.”  
&quot;是的&quot;我说&quot;萨特比尔-贝迪&quot;

He dipped his head, then lurched forward and claimed my case. A moment later, he was charging through the busy airport. I tried to speak with him, but he merely shook his head and said, “Hindi.”  
他低下头，然后猛地向前冲去，夺走了我的箱子。片刻之后，他就冲进了繁忙的机场。我试图和他说话，但他只是摇了摇头，说了句 &quot;印地语&quot;。

He deposited me near the entrance, with the driver of a white car. Then Delhi was outside my window.  
他和一辆白色轿车的司机一起把我送到入口附近。德里就在我的窗外。  
Horns blared—squeaks from mopeds and guttural blasts from black S.U.V.s. Rickshaws, lime green and banana yellow, weaved and jinked, inserting themselves into impossibly slender gaps. I stared out at the heaving city and felt far from myself.  
轻便摩托车发出刺耳的喇叭声，黑色 S.U.V.汽车发出刺耳的鸣笛声。青绿色和香蕉黄色的人力车穿梭往来，在窄得无法再窄的缝隙中穿行。我凝视着这座喧闹的城市，感觉自己离自己越来越远。

I knew from Sukrit that his mother was important in India, a senior government officer. As a young woman, she had risen swiftly through the ranks, and was now one of only a handful of women in the country working at her level.  
我从苏克里特那里得知，他的母亲在印度很重要，是一名高级政府官员。作为一名年轻女性，她迅速晋升为高级官员，现在是印度仅有的几位与她同级的女性之一。  
A decade earlier, the United Nations had sent her to Afghanistan, to Kapisa Province, to oversee the first legislative election in three decades. When she returned home, she was appointed the chief electoral officer in Delhi, one of the most prominent posts in the capital.  
十年前，联合国派她前往阿富汗卡比萨省，监督三十年来的首次立法选举。回国后，她被任命为德里的首席选举官，这是首都最重要的职位之一。  
I imagined her and felt a nervous tremor.  
我想象着她的样子，感到一阵紧张的颤抖。

We arrived at the house, which was simple, two stories, with whitewashed walls and a small garden. The driver carried my case inside, up a flight of stairs and into a large bedroom. The floor was vitrified tile. The bed was very low.  
我们到了那所房子，房子很简单，两层楼，粉刷过的墙壁和一个小花园。司机把我的箱子搬进去，上了一层楼梯，进了一间大卧室。地板是瓷砖铺成的。床很低。  
The driver said, “Madam Bedi will see you at dinner.” Then he was gone.  
司机说：&quot;贝迪夫人晚餐时会来看你。&quot;然后他就走了。

I explored the house, which was quiet. The shades were drawn, and the rooms were dark. I moved down the stairs, through the dining room and into the sitting room, whose furniture was covered in lambent silks that gleamed even in the low light.  
我探索了一下房子，里面很安静。窗帘拉上了，房间里一片漆黑。我走下楼梯，穿过餐厅，来到起居室，起居室的家具上铺着羊羔色的丝绸，即使在微弱的光线下也闪闪发光。  
Everything felt unfamiliar, from the murmur of a language I didn’t know, in the kitchen, to a golden shrine in the stairwell, dedicated to an unknown deity.  
一切都让我感到陌生，从厨房里喃喃自语的我不知道的语言，到楼梯间供奉着未知神灵的金色神龛。

I retreated to my room, felt a chill in my feet as they pressed against the cold tile, and wondered why I had come here. Then I folded myself into the low bed and slept.  
我回到自己的房间，脚踩在冰冷的瓷砖上，感到一阵寒意，不知道自己为什么会来到这里。然后，我把自己折叠到低矮的床上，睡了过去。

Hours later, I awoke to the sound of voices. The sun was gone, and outside my window the sky was a deep purple. I went downstairs and saw that the house was lit and a crowd had gathered in the sitting room—a woman and six men.  
几个小时后，我被声音惊醒。太阳已经落山，窗外的天空一片深紫。我下楼一看，房子里亮着灯，起居室里聚集了一群人--一个女人和六个男人。  
The men wore gray suits; the woman, a long sari, black as onyx, its edge embroidered in a geometric floral pattern of gold brocade. The golden threads flickered, illuminating her movements as she spoke rapidly in Hindi.  
男人们穿着灰色的西装，女人则穿着一件黑色如玛瑙的长纱丽，纱丽的边缘绣着金色锦缎的几何花卉图案。金丝闪烁，照亮了她用印地语快速说话时的动作。

I hovered in the stairwell, unsure what to do. The woman saw me. She stood, crossed the room, and met me at the threshold. “I am Satbir Bedi,” she said. “Welcome.”  
我在楼梯间徘徊，不知如何是好。那个女人看到了我。她站了起来，穿过房间，在门口迎接我。&quot;我是萨特比尔-贝迪，&quot;她说。&quot;欢迎光临

Her speech was fluid. I understood the words, but the rhythm and pitch were new, familiar lyrics set to an unfamiliar melody. She spoke differently than her son: Sukrit had lived for so long in the United States and England, he sounded more like me than he did his mother.  
她说得很流畅。我听得懂她说的话，但节奏和音调都很新，熟悉的歌词配上陌生的旋律。她说话的方式和她儿子不一样：苏克里特在美国和英国生活了很长时间，他听起来更像我，而不是他的母亲。  
She smiled, but I sensed that her mind was with the men. She asked me to wait while she finished her work, then she returned to the sitting room.  
她微笑着，但我感觉到她的心思都在男人身上。她让我等她忙完，然后她回到起居室。

Sukrit had told me that his mother had faced down warlords, and seeing her now, I believed it. She held herself fully erect, the folds of her sari resting over her left arm. There were lines etched into her face; she was not young, but her features were stately.  
苏克里特曾告诉我，他的母亲曾面对过军阀，看到她现在的样子，我相信了。她站得笔直，纱丽的褶皱搭在左臂上。她的脸上刻满了皱纹；她并不年轻，但五官却很端庄。  
Again I felt anxious, and perhaps a little afraid. I wanted this woman to like me. She was one of those people who inspire that wish in others, who give the sense of having lived with great intensity, and of being able to contain the things that have happened to them.  
我再次感到焦虑，也许还有点害怕。我希望这个女人喜欢我。她是那种能激起别人这种愿望的人，她给人的感觉是活得很充实，能够容纳发生在自己身上的事情。

I sat at the dining-room table, with a clear view of the sitting room. Madam Bedi settled herself on a white sofa, and the men arranged themselves around her. She was speaking again, and quickly. The men were nodding.  
我坐在餐桌旁，起居室一览无余。贝迪夫人坐在一张白色沙发上，男人们围着她坐好。她又开始说话了，而且语速很快。男人们都在点头。  
Some spoke, but my impression was that they had come to listen.  
有些人说了话，但我的印象是他们是来倾听的。

Sukrit had explained to me the hierarchical nature of Indian culture—that seniority trumps almost everything, even gender—but seeing it now, I found it difficult to believe. Who was this woman? My notion of India was of a country averse to granting women power.  
苏克里特曾向我解释过印度文化中的等级制度--辈分几乎高于一切，甚至是性别，但现在看到这一切，我觉得很难相信。这个女人是谁？在我的印象中，印度是一个不喜欢赋予女性权力的国家。  
Yet here she sat, cloaked in it.  
然而，她就坐在这里，披着权力的外衣。

The meeting did not last long, then one by one the men left, until finally Madam Bedi was sitting across from me at the dining table. The house was quiet, although we were not alone. Two men served dinner while a woman in the kitchen rolled small knobs of pale dough.  
会议没有持续多久，然后男人们一个接一个地离开，直到最后贝迪夫人坐在我对面的餐桌旁。屋子里很安静，尽管我们并不孤单。两个男人端着晚餐，一个女人在厨房里搓着小面团。  
The dinner was set out in steel bowls—lentils spiced with cumin and coriander, roasted cauliflower, cubes of chicken braised in turmeric. Saffron rice and a thin bread called chapati.  
晚餐用铁碗盛着--用孜然和香菜调味的扁豆、烤花椰菜、姜黄炖鸡块。藏红花米饭和一种叫 chapati 的薄面包。

We ate. Madam Bedi was polite but distant, and I had the nagging impression that she was unsure about me, that there was, hidden behind her eyes, some private doubt. But whatever was bothering her she did not say.  
我们吃了起来。贝迪夫人彬彬有礼，但很疏远，我总觉得她对我不放心，在她的眼神背后，隐藏着一些私人的疑虑。但无论她在烦恼什么，她都没有说出来。

I asked about Afghanistan. She said she had seen bombs detonate in the street and on roadways, bombs likely intended for the U.S. military but which were a constant threat to the U.N. convoy.  
我问起阿富汗的情况。她说，她曾看到炸弹在街上和公路上引爆，这些炸弹很可能是为美军准备的，但对联合国车队构成了持续威胁。  
She said Kapisa was run by warlords, and that she resolved to meet with them if she could, although this proved difficult. Even some of her own Afghan subordinates refused to meet with her, because she was a woman.  
她说，卡比萨是由军阀管理的，她决心如果可能就与他们会面，尽管事实证明这很困难。甚至她自己的一些阿富汗下属也拒绝与她会面，因为她是个女人。

As she spoke, she tore long strips of chapati, which she used to capture the lentils and cauliflower.  
她一边说，一边撕下长长的小面包条，用来装扁豆和花椰菜。

“If the men wouldn’t see you,” I said, “how could you run the election?”  
&quot;我说：&quot;如果男人们不愿意见你，你怎么能参加选举呢？&quot;

“Bollywood,” she said. &quot;宝莱坞&quot;，她说。

“Sorry?” &quot;对不起？&quot;

“The men there loved Bollywood,” she said. “Many had lived in Pakistan; they knew the old cinema. They were crazy about Shah Rukh Khan. When they found out that I had seen him once, when he was shooting a movie, they were delighted to talk to me.”  
&quot;她说：&quot;那里的男人喜欢宝莱坞。&quot;许多人曾在巴基斯坦生活过；他们了解老电影。他们为沙鲁克-汗疯狂。当他们知道我曾见过他一次，当时他正在拍摄一部电影，他们很高兴和我交谈&quot;。

Madam Bedi told me she sent to Delhi for a case of Bollywood CDs and DVDs, which she handed out to drivers and assistants and interpreters. They were grateful; they began to feed her information. They would tell her which roads to take and when.  
贝迪女士告诉我，她去德里买了一箱宝莱坞 CD 和 DVD，分发给司机、助理和翻译。他们很感激，开始向她提供信息。他们会告诉她什么时候该走哪条路。  
They would say, “Thursday you must stay home. Thursday you must not travel at all.” She said Bollywood saved her life.  
他们会说，&quot;星期四你必须呆在家里。&quot;周四你不能出门&quot;她说宝莱坞救了她的命。

She shared these stories pleasantly but with an air of formality. She was being polite, that was all.  
她愉快地分享着这些故事，但语气中充满了拘谨。她只是出于礼貌，仅此而已。

The next day, when I dragged myself from bed at what would have been four in the morning in London, Madam Bedi was already gone, but she had arranged a breakfast for me of curried vegetables, which I tried to eat despite the spices.  
第二天，当我从床上爬起来时，在伦敦应该是凌晨四点，贝迪女士已经离开了，但她为我安排了咖喱蔬菜早餐。  
She had left a driver, with instructions to take me wherever I wanted to go, and another man, young, with thick black hair shoved rakishly to one side, to tend to me through the day.  
她留下了一个司机，嘱咐他带我去我想去的地方，还有一个年轻的男人，一头浓密的黑发斜披在一边，负责照顾我一整天。  
The young man appeared every few hours, with a silver tray on which stood a cup of black tea, which was bitter but also sweet, with flavors of cardamom and ginger.  
这个年轻人每隔几个小时就会出现一次，手里端着一个银盘，盘子里放着一杯红茶，苦中带甜，有豆蔻和生姜的味道。

That night, at dinner, Madam Bedi asked what I preferred for breakfast. She said that in her experience Americans could not tolerate curried flavors in the morning. I asked for oatmeal, and the next morning a warm bowl of it appeared on the silver tray, next to the black tea.  
那天晚餐时，贝迪夫人问我早餐喜欢吃什么。她说，根据她的经验，美国人早上无法忍受咖喱口味。我要了一碗燕麦粥，第二天早上，一碗温热的燕麦粥出现在银盘上，旁边还有红茶。

Those first days, I explored the city, walking alone through the vastness of the Red Fort, touching the rough sandstone of Humayun’s Tomb, placing a covering over my head so I could sit in the great square and look up at the Jama Masjid Mosque, with its bulbous domes of white marble.  
最初几天，我探索这座城市，独自走过红堡的广阔，触摸胡马雍陵粗糙的砂岩，盖上头巾，坐在大广场上仰望贾玛清真寺，白色大理石的圆球形穹顶。  
I tried to take it in, the grandness of India, its rich architecture, its dramatic history. But even as I walked through those splendid places, my mind returned to the house and the woman who lived in it.  
我试图将印度的壮观、丰富的建筑、戏剧性的历史尽收眼底。但就在我走过这些辉煌的地方时，我的思绪又回到了那所房子和住在里面的女人身上。

After I had been in Delhi for perhaps a week, Madam Bedi took me to see her office. I travelled with her in the white car to an expansive plaza of civic-looking buildings.  
我在德里待了大概一周之后，贝迪夫人带我去参观她的办公室。我和她一起坐着白色的汽车，来到一个由市政建筑组成的宽阔广场。  
We stepped into her suite, and again I observed the gravitational pull of Madam Bedi, the way eyes were drawn in her direction, the way the staff fell into orbit around her.  
我们走进她的套间，我再次观察到贝迪夫人的引力，她的目光被吸引到她的方向，工作人员都围着她转。  
There were men and women both, but it was the behavior of the men that shocked me: when she stood, they revolved around her like satellites; when she walked, they trailed behind her in a comet tail. Never in my life had I seen anything like it, a woman waited on by a dozen men.  
他们有男有女，但令我震惊的是男人们的行为：当她站立时，他们像卫星一样围绕着她旋转；当她行走时，他们像彗尾一样跟在她身后。我一生中从未见过这样的场面，一个女人被十几个男人伺候着。  
I had not seen it in Idaho, and I had not seen it in Cambridge. Neither the religious nor the secular world had shown me the likeness of a female king. But here was one.  
我在爱达荷州没见过，在剑桥也没见过。无论是宗教世界还是世俗世界，都没有向我展示过女国王的模样。但这里就有一个。

I wanted to live a grateful life. I wanted to love my mother and father the way Sukrit loved Satbir, without bitterness. I believed it should be possible to forge that love. I thought I could choose what to feel. I thought this was the meaning of self-mastery.  
我想过感恩的生活。我想像 Sukrit 爱 Satbir 那样爱我的母亲和父亲，没有怨恨。我相信这种爱是可以建立的。我以为我可以选择自己的感受。我以为这就是自我超越的意义。

My second week in Delhi, I began a gratitude journal. The subject I chose was my father, because it was toward him that I held the most anger.  
在德里的第二个星期，我开始写感恩日记。我选择的主题是我的父亲，因为我对他的愤怒最多。  
I wanted to purge that anger from myself, so every morning for a week, in blue ink, I wrote out lists, thanking my father for his contributions to my life.  
我想消除自己的愤怒，所以在一周的时间里，我每天早上都用蓝色墨水写下清单，感谢父亲为我的生活所做的贡献。

*Dear Dad. Thank you for working so I could have food and electricity.  
亲爱的爸爸谢谢你工作，让我有饭吃，有电用。*

*Dear Dad. Thank you for teaching me to be independent.  
亲爱的爸爸感谢您教会我独立。*

*Dear Dad. Thank you for taking us to Arizona.  
亲爱的爸爸谢谢你带我们去亚利桑那州。*

I remember the result of this experiment. It was a disaster. When I reread what I had written, I did not feel grateful; I felt enraged.  
我记得这次实验的结果这是一场灾难。当我重读自己写的东西时，我没有感激之情，反而感到愤怒。

I remembered those trips to Arizona, to Salome, with its foreign landscape of red dirt and green saguaro cacti, where Grandma and Grandpa, my father’s parents, fled each December to escape the Idaho winter.  
我还记得那些去亚利桑那州的旅行，去萨洛米，那里有红色的泥土和绿色的萨瓜罗仙人掌，每年 12 月，祖母和祖父，也就是我父亲的父母，都会去那里躲避爱达荷州的冬天。  
After one visit there, when I was nine, we crashed coming home because Dad had insisted that my brother Tyler, who was seventeen, drive our blue station wagon through the night. Dad was tired. He wanted to sleep, so the car was silent. No one spoke, not for hours.  
在我九岁那年，有一次去了那里之后，我们在回家的路上撞车了，因为爸爸坚持让我十七岁的哥哥泰勒开着我们的蓝色旅行车彻夜赶路。爸爸累了。他想睡觉，所以车里一片寂静。没有人说话，几个小时都没有。  
Then, around six in the morning, after Tyler had been driving in silence for six hours, he fell asleep and the car drifted over the yellow line.  
早上六点左右，泰勒在沉默中开了六个小时后，他睡着了，车子越过了黄线。

The station wagon left the road, smashing through two utility poles of thick cedar, one after the other, and snapping them. The poles collapsed, dropping the power lines to the ground, and still the car kept going.  
旅行车驶离了公路，撞断了两根粗大的雪松电线杆，一根接一根地折断。电线杆倒塌，电线掉落在地上，而车仍在继续前行。  
It came to rest, finally, in a field, when it hit a row-crop tractor. Our mother was sitting in the front seat. On impact, she was thrown into the windshield.  
最后，它停在了田野里，撞上了一台行作物拖拉机。我们的母亲当时坐在前座上。撞击时，她被甩到了挡风玻璃上。  
Hours later, two goose eggs had formed on her head, one above each eye, swollen and enormous, darkening from pink to purple. She was disoriented and seemed to have suffered a brain injury, but Dad didn’t take her to the hospital because he didn’t believe in them.  
几个小时后，她的头上长出了两个鹅蛋，一个在眼睛上方，肿得很大，颜色从粉红色变成了紫色。她神志不清，似乎脑部受伤，但爸爸没有带她去医院，因为他不相信医院。

Four years later, again we drove to Arizona, and again we crashed on the return. It was January. There was a blizzard that night, a whiteout in southern Utah, the kind of chalk-white frenzy in which you can’t see more than a few inches in the dark.  
四年后，我们再次驱车前往亚利桑那州，又一次在回程中撞车。当时是一月。那天晚上下了一场暴雪，犹他州南部一片白茫茫，那种白垩色的狂风暴雪，在黑暗中你根本看不清几英寸的范围。  
The beam of our headlights reflected off the thick flurries, increasing our blindness. Every other car on the road pulled over, to wait out the storm. These were Utah drivers, experienced with snow. But they knew when to surrender to nature.  
车灯的光束反射在厚厚的雪花上，增加了我们的盲目性。路上的每辆车都把车停在路边，等待暴风雪的到来。这些都是犹他州的司机，对雪很有经验。但他们知道什么时候该向大自然投降。

Cartoon by Sara Lautman 萨拉-劳特曼的漫画

Dad persisted, pressing his foot down hard on the accelerator, shooting through the whiteout in our rickety old Astro van at sixty-five miles an hour. I was in the back, lying with my mother on a futon, because Dad had removed the seats and seat belts.  
爸爸坚持不懈，脚狠狠地踩着油门，开着我们那辆摇摇晃晃的老式阿斯特罗面包车，以每小时六十五英里的速度穿过白茫茫的夜空。我坐在后排，和妈妈一起躺在蒲团上，因为爸爸已经拆掉了座椅和安全带。  
From where I lay, I could see my brother Richard’s left hand, clenching the armrest of the passenger seat. Later, Richard would tell me that it was a relief when the van finally left the road. “At least it was over,” he said.  
从我躺着的地方，我可以看到哥哥理查德的左手紧紧抓住副驾驶座位的扶手。后来，理查德告诉我，当面包车最终驶离公路时，我才松了一口气。&quot;他说：&quot;至少一切都结束了。

The van rolled, I don’t know how many times or if it was just once. I was knocked out, my neck snapped backward, pushed sideways into some malefic diagonal so that some days later the vertebrae froze, and I could not turn my head either to the left or the right.  
面包车翻滚了，我不知道翻滚了多少次，也不知道是不是只有一次。我被撞得晕了过去，脖子向后折，被推到了某个邪恶的对角线上，以至于几天后脊椎骨都僵住了，我的头既不能向左也不能向右。  
My mother treated the paralysis with homeopathy and oils, blue tansy and German camomile, but it didn’t improve.  
母亲用顺势疗法和精油、蓝丹参和德国洋甘菊治疗我的瘫痪，但情况并没有好转。  
I remained paralyzed—my brothers called me Popsicle—for some weeks, until one evening, when I was standing at the kitchen sink, washing dishes, one of my older brothers took hold of my head and wrenched it, cracking my neck.  
直到有一天晚上，我站在厨房的水槽边洗碗，我的一个哥哥抱住我的头，用力一扭，我的脖子就断了。  
The treatment was harsh, frightening even, but it worked. The next day I could turn my head.  
这种做法很残忍，甚至让人害怕，但它起了作用。第二天，我就能转头了。

*Dear Dad. Thank you for taking us to Arizona.* I read the sentence again, reaching for gratitude but finding nothing in me that could be bent into that shape. Then I felt thwarted, a failure. I did not want to live an angry life.  
\_亲爱的爸爸。谢谢你带我们去亚利桑那州。\_我又读了一遍这句话，想要表达感激之情，却发现自己身上没有任何东西可以弯曲成那样。于是我感到挫败和失败。我不想过愤怒的生活。  
I did not want to be the hag, the ingrate, the twitching, bitter crow who never lets go of anything. I wanted a bigger life than that.  
我不想成为怨妇，不想成为忘恩负义的人，也不想成为那只抽搐的、痛苦的、什么都不放过的乌鸦。我想要更精彩的生活。

I wanted to love my parents. I knew, even then, that love is what brings a sense of expansion to life. I wanted a life of beauty and gratitude, arms outstretched and hopeful. Would this be forever out of my reach? How can a person transcend what has happened to them?  
我想爱我的父母。即使在那时，我就知道，爱能给生命带来扩张感。我想要一种美丽和感恩的生活，伸开双臂，充满希望。这会是我永远无法企及的吗？一个人如何才能超越发生在自己身上的一切？

The car crashes were over, finished. They were in the past. Could I not now forget them? Be grateful for the good and bury the bad? Could I not choose a forgiving life? Sometimes, I thought I could forgive if the past could be acknowledged, even partially.  
车祸结束了，结束了。它们已经成为过去。难道我现在不能忘记它们吗？对好的事物心存感激，对坏的事物埋藏心底？难道我不能选择一种宽容的生活吗？有时，我想，如果过去能够被承认，哪怕是部分承认，我也可以原谅。  
But my parents denied everything. The isolation. The injuries. All of it.  
但我的父母否认了一切。隔离受伤所有的一切

I wrote the sentences again. Then a third time. And a fourth. I copied the page five times a day until the notebook was filled. I believed, truly believed, that I could make myself feel about the past whatever I wanted to feel.  
我又写了一遍然后第三次第四遍我每天抄写五遍，直到笔记本被填满。我相信，真的相信，我可以让自己对过去有任何我想要的感觉。

What I wanted was to become a different person, who had lived a different life with a different father. I thought I could become that person through force of will. Happiness could be manufactured. Gratitude drawn from a schematic and replicated. *I will eradicate my rage*, I wrote in my notebook. *I will write, say, and think grateful thoughts until the words become true*.  
我想要的是成为一个与众不同的人，与不同的父亲过着不同的生活。我以为我可以通过意志力变成那样的人。幸福是可以制造出来的。感激之情可以从示意图中提取并复制。*我要消除我的愤怒*，我在笔记本上写道。*我要写下、说出、思考感恩的想法，直到这些话成为现实*。

No one had ever told me that delusion is not a nutrient, that you cannot build a true future from a false past.  
从来没有人告诉过我，妄想不是养分，你不可能从虚假的过去建立起真实的未来。

Madam Bedi had divorced her husband.  
贝迪女士已与丈夫离婚。

I knew from Sukrit that it had happened years ago, and that it had been both necessary and impossible. The marriage had been dismal and harsh, but marriage was inescapable for a woman at that time. An Indian woman, let alone a public woman, simply did not divorce her husband.  
我从苏克里特那里得知，这是多年前发生的事情，既是必要的，也是不可能的。这段婚姻很不幸，也很残酷，但在当时，婚姻对一个女人来说是不可避免的。一个印度女人，更不用说一个公众妇女，根本不会与丈夫离婚。  
Yet there was no husband living in that house.  
然而，那所房子里并没有丈夫。

One night, after I had been in Delhi for two weeks, Madam Bedi asked how Sukrit had been at Cambridge. I said that he had done well in everything except cooking.  
我在德里待了两周后的一天晚上，贝迪夫人问我苏克里特在剑桥过得怎么样。我说他除了做饭之外，其他方面都很好。

“Once he tried to make biryani in my kitchen,” I said.  
&quot;有一次，他想在我的厨房里做印度烤肉，&quot;我说。

“I know,” she said. “He called to ask how much garlic. It was two in the morning in Delhi.”  
&quot;我知道，&quot;她说。&quot;他打电话来问大蒜多少钱。当时是德里的凌晨两点。&quot;

“You told him three cloves.”  
&quot;你告诉他三瓣&quot;

“I did.” &quot;是的&quot;

“You told him three cloves,” I said. “But you did not tell him what a clove is.”  
&quot;你告诉他三颗丁香，&quot;我说&quot;但你没有告诉他丁香是什么&quot;

Her chapati fell from her fingers and she looked at me, mouth sagging, eyes fixed.  
她的小面包从手指上掉了下来，她看着我，嘴角下垂，目光呆滞。

I nodded. “He put in three heads of garlic.”  
我点点头&quot;他放了三头大蒜&quot;

She laughed then. The sound was girlish, joyful, and I realized that her features, when at ease, were impish.  
这时，她笑了。她的笑声很少女，很快乐，我意识到她的五官在放松的时候是不怀好意的。

“He didn’t even peel them,” I said. “They went straight into the pot, skins and all.”  
&quot;他甚至没有剥皮，&quot;我说。&quot;它们直接就下锅了，连皮一起&quot;

Now her head jerked back and she laughed with her whole self.  
现在，她的头向后一仰，整个人都笑了。

I told her that I had shouted at him for ten minutes. I told him that no recipe in the history of time, not in any culture, alive or dead, had ever called for three heads of unpeeled garlic, but he insisted that if the garlic needed peeling, his mother would have told him.  
我告诉她，我对他喊了十分钟。我告诉他，在历史上，在任何文化中，不管是活着的还是死去的，都没有哪种食谱需要三头未剥皮的大蒜，但他坚持说，如果大蒜需要剥皮，他妈妈会告诉他的。

Madam Bedi was looking ahead now, gazing into nothing. She was smiling. She loved her son, and she loved poking fun at her son.  
贝迪夫人现在正目视前方，凝神静气。她在微笑。她爱她的儿子，也爱拿她的儿子开玩笑。

The next time I looked up, her expression had narrowed. She turned toward me and finally asked the question that, later, I understood had been on her mind from the beginning. She asked if Sukrit and I were romantic. I said no.  
下一次我抬起头时，她的表情收敛了。她转过身来，终于问了一个问题，后来我才明白，她从一开始就在想这个问题。她问我和苏克里特是否浪漫。我说不是。  
She was still staring at me, a sadness pulling her lips downward. This was the obstacle, the wall between us: she did not want her son to marry a white woman and disappear forever to America.  
她仍然盯着我，嘴角向下牵动着一丝悲伤。这就是我们之间的障碍和隔阂：她不希望她的儿子娶一个白人女人，然后永远消失在美国。

“I love your son,” I said. “But I love him like a brother.”  
&quot;我爱你的儿子，&quot;我说。&quot;但我爱他就像爱我的兄弟&quot;

She peered into me and the knowledge seemed to settle. “Then you are my daughter,” she said, and picked up her chapati.  
她注视着我，似乎明白了什么。&quot;那你就是我的女儿，&quot;她说，然后拿起了她的小面包。

Our dialogue shifted after that. The wall had come down, and I began to feel at home there.  
此后，我们的对话发生了转变。墙倒了，我开始有了家的感觉。

Days later, Madam Bedi asked if I would go with her to an engagement party. On the day of the party, she arrived home early to dress and waved me into her bedroom.  
几天后，贝迪女士问我是否愿意和她一起去参加订婚派对。宴会当天，她早早地回到家梳妆打扮，然后招手让我进她的卧室。  
Laid out on her bed were a dozen splendid fabrics, rich silks and soft chiffons, some embroidered with silk threading, others dense with beadwork. She insisted that I try each one and in the end selected for me a *lehenga*, which was a dense shade of marigold. The ensemble consisted of a long beaded skirt with a matching silk blouse, or *choli*, and an intricate shawl, which she folded into a neat drapery and pinned across my body. Satisfied, finally, with the shape of the cloth, she removed the earrings from her own ears, two gold shells, and folded them into my palm. Then she called to the driver.  
她的床上摆放着十几种华丽的面料，有丰富的丝绸和柔软的雪纺，有的上面绣着丝线，有的上面密密麻麻地缀满了珠子。她坚持让我一一试穿，最后为我挑选了一件\_金盏花色的长裙。这套服装包括一条长长的珠饰裙子和一件配套的丝绸上衣（即 *choli* ），还有一条复杂的披肩，她把披肩叠成整齐的帷幔，用别针别在我的身上。最后，她对布料的形状感到满意，从自己的耳朵上取下耳环--两个金贝壳，折叠好放在我的手掌上。然后，她叫来了司机。\_

The party was immense, hundreds of people pressed into a vast and glittering ballroom. The women shimmered; the men stood stoically. A path was cut through the crowd, through which passed Madam Bedi, nodding and smiling, wearing her elfin grin.  
宴会规模宏大，数百人挤在宽敞闪亮的舞厅里。女人们闪闪发光，男人们站得笔直。一条小路穿过人群，贝迪女士从小路走过，她点头微笑，露出精灵般的笑容。

I remember little of the party. What I remember is what came after. There was traffic in the city that night, and for an hour we sat together in the back of the white car, waiting for the knot of tail-lights to unpick itself. While we waited, we traded stories of Sukrit.  
我对聚会的印象不深。我记得的是后来发生的事情。那天晚上，城市里堵车，我们一起坐在白色汽车的后座上，等了一个小时，等着尾灯的结解开。在等待的过程中，我们谈论着苏克里特的故事。  
She never tired of these stories.  
她对这些故事百听不厌。

I do not remember how she came to tell me of her divorce.  
我不记得她是如何告诉我她离婚的。

She had chosen to get married young, to a non-Sikh. Her father had opposed the match but Satbir, ever willful, ever testing the limits of her autonomy, had married the man anyway. Quickly the marriage had become deranged.  
她选择在年轻时结婚，嫁给一个非锡克教徒。她的父亲反对这桩婚事，但一向任性的萨特比尔不断试探自己的自主权，还是嫁给了那个男人。很快，这段婚姻就变得不正常了。  
Her star was rising, and her husband resented the steady accumulation of her successes, her recognition. Outside the home, she was powerful, but inside her status as a wife gave him absolute power.  
她的事业如日中天，而她的丈夫却对她不断积累的成功和得到的认可心怀怨恨。在家庭之外，她是强大的，但在家庭之内，她作为妻子的身份给了他绝对的权力。

Satbir told me that she had come close to breaking. The marriage had been unendurable. She had gone to her father and told him that she must have a divorce.  
萨特比尔告诉我，她差点就崩溃了。这段婚姻已经无法忍受。她去找父亲，告诉他她必须离婚。

She loved her father; their relationship had been repaired since she had married her husband, had become something beautiful that she valued. She needed his approval to do this radical thing. But he would not give it. He said that she could not divorce her husband.  
她爱她的父亲；自从她与丈夫结婚后，他们的关系得到了修复，变得更加美好，这是她所珍视的。她需要得到他的同意才能做这件激进的事。但他不同意。他说她不能和丈夫离婚。  
She had a public name to uphold, a family name. She could not flout social mores so flagrantly. She would be pilloried in public, denounced in private.  
她要维护一个公众的名声，一个家庭的名声。她不能如此明目张胆地蔑视社会风气。她会被当众指责，私下告发。

She divorced her husband.  
她与丈夫离婚了。

“How did you do it?” I said. I hadn’t meant to interrupt. “How did you know you wouldn’t lose everything?”  
&quot;你是怎么做到的？&quot;我说。我不是故意打断你的&quot;你怎么知道你不会失去一切？&quot;

She turned toward me, but her face was shadowed. When she spoke I could not see her. I heard only a clear voice in the dark.  
她转过身来，但脸上有阴影。她说话时，我看不见她。我只听到黑暗中一个清晰的声音。

“I didn’t,” she said. “I did not know what would happen. I only knew that I had to do it. The marriage was over. I could not live the rest of my life in its dead body.”  
&quot;我不知道。&quot;她说。&quot;我不知道会发生什么。我只知道我必须这么做。婚姻结束了。我不能在它的尸体上度过余生&quot;

She looked out the window, and I saw the lights of the city pass across her face. Her features were pulled downward, her expression doleful. She had suffered; I could see the suffering written on her face.  
她望着窗外，我看到城市的灯光从她脸上掠过。她的五官向下拉，表情忧郁。她遭受过苦难，我可以看到她脸上写满了苦难。  
But even as I watched her, and witnessed her remembering her misery, I felt the unmistakable presence of strength. She sat calmly, her hands in her lap, her eyes fixed on the outside world.  
但就在我注视着她，目睹她回忆起自己的苦难时，我感受到了无以言表的力量。她平静地坐着，双手放在膝盖上，眼睛注视着外面的世界。  
She wore a simple white kurta, crocheted, a delicate textile, with an orange stole folded in precise lines over her right shoulder.  
她穿着一件简单的白色坎肩，是钩针编织而成的，质地细腻，右肩披着一条橙色披肩，披肩折叠的线条非常精确。

I thought of the life she had chosen for herself—of her ceaseless insistence on self-rule. Of her willingness to risk everything, even the bond with a father she loved, rather than surrender herself to the rule of another.  
我想到了她为自己选择的生活--她对自我统治的不懈坚持。她甘愿牺牲一切，甚至牺牲与她所爱的父亲的亲情，也不愿屈服于他人的统治。

I wanted to know how she had done it.  
我想知道她是怎么做到的。

She said that she had travelled to visit a friend in Varanasi, the city in the north where the dead are sent to be burned. She had arrived in a weakened state, half wrecked, hardly able to hold herself together. But in that city she found strength.  
她说她去瓦拉纳西看望一个朋友，瓦拉纳西是北方的一个城市，死人都会被送到那里烧掉。她到达时身体虚弱，半身不遂，几乎无法支撑自己。但在那个城市，她找到了力量。

“How?” I said. “How did you keep from breaking?”  
&quot;怎么做？&quot;我说&quot;你是怎么不崩溃的？&quot;

There was tension in my chest. I needed an answer. My question was not about her, I knew that; it was about my own stuckness.  
我的胸口很紧张。我需要一个答案。我知道，我的问题不是关于她，而是关于我自己的困顿。

“I broke,” she said. “That is how I kept from breaking.”  
&quot;我崩溃了，&quot;她说。&quot;我就是这样才没有崩溃&quot;

She said that one night, sitting outside the city, on the banks of the River Ganges, watching the smoke rise, she had come to pieces in the arms of her friend. She had wept, bitterly and with such violence she thought her chest might tear open.  
她说，有一天晚上，她坐在城外的恒河岸边，看着袅袅炊烟升起，她在朋友的怀抱中崩溃了。她哭了，哭得很伤心，哭得很猛烈 她以为自己的胸膛会被撕裂

“I succumbed,” she said. “I stopped denying the wounds and I felt them, felt their width and breadth. Pain can be clarifying. If you are able to feel it, the pain itself—the true knowledge of what doing nothing is costing you—will tell you what to do.”  
&quot;我屈服了，&quot;她说。&quot;我不再否认伤口，而是去感受它们，感受它们的宽度和广度。痛苦可以让人清醒。如果你能感受到它，痛苦本身--真正了解无所作为的代价--就会告诉你该怎么做。&quot;

I looked out the window, feeling disappointed and empty. This was not the answer I had come for. I wanted something else, some other formula or technique, something more recognizable as strength. I wanted her to tell me how I could live my life and never succumb to anything.  
我望着窗外，感到失望和空虚。这不是我想要的答案。我想要的是其他的东西，一些其他的公式或技巧，一些更容易辨认的力量。我想让她告诉我，我怎样才能生活下去，永远不屈服于任何事物。

We returned to the house and I went quietly to bed, where I lay awake, staring at the dark. I fell asleep remembering the story, the words and the way she had said them: *I travelled to Varanasi. I watched the dead burn and the smoke rise. I was destroyed by my life. I succumbed, and was destroyed.*  
我们回到家里，我静静地躺在床上。我记住了这个故事，记住了她说的话和她说的方式，就这样睡着了：*我去了瓦拉纳西。我看着死人被烧死，看着浓烟升起。我的生活摧毁了我。我屈服了，也毁灭了。*

I dreamed that night and for many nights after. I dreamed of Satbir, and of myself. The dream was stylized, like a fairy tale or fable, in which the kingdom must be saved by a lone wanderer or journeyman, who voyages to faraway places, seeking wisdom.  
那晚我做梦了，之后的许多个夜晚也是如此。我梦到了萨特比尔，也梦到了我自己。梦是风格化的，就像童话或寓言故事，其中王国必须由一个孤独的流浪者或旅行者来拯救，他远航到遥远的地方，寻求智慧。

In the dream, I travel to a distant land and ask the woman king: What is the source of your power?  
在梦中，我来到遥远的国度，向女国王发问：你力量的源泉是什么？

Weeping, she says. The source of my power is that I weep.  
她说，哭泣。我的力量源泉就是我的哭泣。

She has given me the answer. But I do not believe her. ♦  
她已经给了我答案。但我不相信她。♦
